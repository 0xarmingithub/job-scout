# CV best practices

## 0. First principles

1. **The master content file is not a CV.** It holds everything. Each
   application gets a subset. Never send the master.
2. **Tailor to the posting, not to a template.** Map what the posting needs onto
   what you really did.
3. **Outcome over activity.** Readers care what changed because you were there.
4. **Truth over polish.** An inflated claim surfaces in an interview. A tool that
   invents a number can end an application.
5. **A recruiter's first pass is seconds.** It lands on the summary, the top
   bullet of the latest role, and the skills section.
6. **ATS is real but secondary.** Get past the keyword filter, then write for the
   person who reads next.

## 1. The master content file

Keep one living file with every role, every phrasing, every project, every
certification, every tool and how deeply you know it, and every number with a
note of how it was measured. Add new wins within a couple of days of finishing
them, while the details are fresh. Keep personal details in their own section and
pull them in only when a posting needs them.

## 2. Reading a posting

- Separate must-have from nice-to-have by the posting's own words.
- Find the real job under the title: what problem is the hire meant to solve?
- Pair every acronym with its full term, because matching is literal.
- Note impossible asks and unusual demands as red flags.

## 3. Gap analysis

For each requirement, classify your coverage as strong, weak, transferable or
none. A transferable claim needs a one-sentence mechanism. If you cannot write
it, it is a gap. Real gaps stay off the CV. If real gaps cover more than about a
third of the must-haves, consider building the skill before applying.

## 4. The summary

- Two to four lines. Years, top strengths, what you offer.
- Open with a noun phrase that matches the posting's role language and that your
  titles and bullets can support.
- No objectives ("seeking a challenging role"). No first-person pronouns.

## 5. Experience bullets

- Action verb, what, how, quantified outcome.
- Lead each role with its most relevant bullet.
- Three to six bullets for recent roles, fewer for older ones.
- Give two similar recent roles comparable depth when you have material for both.

## 6. Numbers

- Every number is one you can explain: where it came from and how it was measured.
- Never round up, never estimate silently, never generalise.
- A strong plain bullet beats a bullet with a number you cannot defend.
- If a bullet has no number, ask what could be counted: users, teams, systems,
  hours saved, incidents avoided.

## 7. Skills section

- Group by the categories the posting groups by, and lead with the one it
  stresses.
- Use the posting's exact tool names when they are true for you.
- Four groups of four to six items beat forty keywords.
- Do not list what you cannot discuss for a minute.
- Never attach "familiar with", "basic" or "some exposure" to a skill the posting
  lists as core. Say what you did, or leave it out and cover it in a letter or
  interview.
- Name specific mechanisms rather than umbrella terms when the posting asks for
  specifics.

## 8. Applicant tracking systems

- Standard headings: Professional Summary, Skills, Experience, Education, Certifications.
- Reverse chronological.
- No layout tables, and no images or icons that carry meaning.
- Acronym and full term together at least once.
- Check the text layer of your PDF. If you cannot read the extracted text top to
  bottom in the right order, neither can the system.
- Keep a plain-text variant for portals that parse badly or ask you to paste.
- Some portals parse Word documents better than PDFs. Check the posting URL.
- Use a plain file name without spaces or special characters.

## 9. What does not belong on a CV

- A photo, unless your market expects one.
- Date of birth, marital status, ID numbers.
- Pay history or expectations.
- A generic objective statement.
- An explanation of a gap in dates. Use a letter or a call.
- Skills you cannot defend.

## 10. Career pivots

When the target role is adjacent, not identical:
- Open with the target role's language, supported by your most transferable strengths.
- Translate domain language the new audience will not recognise.
- Acknowledge the pivot honestly in a letter or on the first call. Do not disguise it.

## 11. Iteration

- Change one thing at a time when you can, so you learn what worked.
- Record every application's outcome. A pattern across ten applications is worth
  more than an opinion about one.

## 12. Interviews

The tailored CV is also your script. For each top bullet, prepare a short story:
situation, task, action, result, and what you learned. Prepare honest answers for
the gaps. Prepare questions that show you read the posting and looked at the company.

## 13. Cover letters

Write one only when the posting asks for it or you choose to. First person,
one page, it argues fit rather than repeating the CV, and it is the one place to
address a single real gap honestly.

## 14. Titles

The job title on your CV is the exact title you held. Positioning belongs in the
summary and the bullets.

## 15. Anti-patterns

- Sending the same CV to every posting.
- Letting a tool invent a metric or a skill.
- Stretching "transferable" past honesty.
- Layout tricks that break parsing.
- Long prose in the summary.
- Retyping shared sections (education, certifications) by hand into each
  application, so one copy silently misses a later correction.
